    A normal 95% interval is only right if you look once. The always valid one, from the mixture sequential test, stays right however often you check, and it only ever gets narrower.

    If you can fix the number of looks and the most players up front, a group sequential test splits the 5% across those looks instead. O'Brien Fleming keeps the early bars high, so it almost never stops on noise and loses very little power. Pocock stops sooner on average but needs more players to keep the same power.

    The data has no timestamps, so this puts the players in a random order and replays it. It shows how much the stopping point depends on luck. It can't show anything that depends on real time.

        Validation
        How I know the math is right
        
      
        I tested every method in abkit on simulated experiments where I knew the true answer. 91 of 127 rows pass. Of the 36 that fail, 18 are mistakes I ran on purpose to show what goes wrong, like counting one person's many visits as many people. The other 18 are real limits, and the note on each one says why.
        
      
        This page runs the same math in JavaScript. On abkit's 197 golden test cases it gives the same 2,383 numbers as the Python library, each to at least 9 significant digits. That includes the two fixes in the always valid test, for huge means and for early looks where one group has no spread yet. The planned looks part is checked the same way against abkit's group sequential code, 1,406 numbers over 48 designs and 30 replays, also to at least 9 digits.
